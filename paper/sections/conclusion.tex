\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Target Environments}

Our approach delivers maximum impact in constraint-based environments where inversions are cheap and commitments are expensive. \textbf{Arithmetized systems} (R1CS, Plonkish/SCS) benefit because Schwartz-Zippel evaluation is efficient and field inversions cost only 2C. \textbf{Recursive proof systems} benefit most directly: the 44\% commitment reduction compounds at each recursion level, since each commitment in the inner circuit becomes a constraint in the outer circuit. \textbf{ZKVMs} (RISC Zero, SP1, Jolt) benefit naturally as they express computation as constraint systems compatible with our polynomial framework. \textbf{Calldata-constrained blockchains} (Ethereum L2s, BitVM) also benefit, as fewer commitments means smaller proof transcripts.

\subsection{Implementation and Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:main_performance} presents benchmark results from our gnark implementation of the polynomial ring toolkit, measured on BN254 2-pairing verification. Our theoretical analysis (Section~\ref{sec:op:comp:our}) predicted 46\% constraint reduction and 75\% commitment reduction per zero-bit step. The end-to-end results confirm both directions of saving: \textbf{25\% SCS constraint reduction} (1,447,170~$\to$~1,081,733) and \textbf{44\% commitment reduction} (539,884~$\to$~303,856).

The commitment reduction exceeds the per-step theoretical prediction because the batching benefit compounds across non-zero bits (which have two line functions per pair), the Frobenius correction step, and the final exponentiation---all contributing to the $O(k)$ vs.\ $O(k \cdot n)$ scaling advantage. The constraint reduction is more modest because constraints encode remainder range-checks (one per multiplication), which our scheme does not eliminate; what we remove is the per-multiplication quotient and carry commitment overhead.

\subsection{Comparison with State of the Art}

Gnark is currently the industry-standard framework for field emulation in arithmetized circuits. Its baseline for BN254 2-pairing verification---1,447,170 SCS constraints and 539,884 commitments---represents the current state of the art. Our polynomial ring toolkit, integrated directly into gnark's \texttt{emulated} package, reduces these to 1,081,733 constraints and 303,856 commitments.

The key structural difference is that gnark's baseline verifies each emulated $\Fe{12}$ multiplication individually inside the circuit (one deferred check per multiplication, one committed quotient and carry per check), while our scheme batches all polynomial verification into a single two-round IOP with an accumulated quotient. This is why commitment overhead drops by 44\%---we collapse $O(k \cdot n)$ individual quotient commitments into a single $Q_{\mathrm{acc}}$ per ring group---while constraint counts drop by a more modest 25\%: remainder range-checks are retained as they are needed for soundness.

The Garaga library~\cite{grg} provided an earlier proof of concept for this technique in Cairo/Starknet in 2024, but lacked a systematic comparison against an industry baseline. This gnark implementation provides that comparison, establishing the polynomial ring toolkit as a concrete, measurable improvement over the state of the art.

\subsection{Final Remarks}

We have shown that choosing the right granularity for polynomial identity testing---entire Miller loop steps rather than individual multiplications---yields substantial and measurable improvements. The key technique, collapsing a composite multi-operand product into a single Euclidean division check with batched quotient accumulation, is simple to implement and broadly applicable to other multi-step cryptographic computations in arithmetized environments.

Our polynomial ring toolkit is implemented in gnark's \texttt{emulated} package and is available as a reusable, parameterised library for any emulated field and modulus polynomial. We hope this work encourages further exploration of polynomial batching granularity as a design axis for constraint-system implementations.
