\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Target Environments}

Our polynomial batching approach delivers maximum impact in constraint-based environments where traditional optimization strategies fall short. These environments share key characteristics that make our techniques particularly effective:

\textbf{Arithmetized Computation Models:} Systems like Cairo VM, R1CS, and Plonkish arithmetization benefit most from our approach. In these environments, field inversions cost nearly the same as multiplications (2C vs 1C), while polynomial verification through Schwartz-Zippel becomes remarkably efficient. Our batching transforms hundreds of individual field operations into single polynomial verifications.

\textbf{Calldata-Constrains Blockchains:} Ethereum Layer 2s, Bitcoin sidechains, and other blockchain VMs impose limits on transaction calldata. Our 77\% reduction in commitment overhead directly translates to fitting more proofs within transaction limits—enabling applications previously impossible due to size constraints.

\textbf{Recursive Proof Systems:} In recursive SNARK/STARK compositions where pairings verify inner proofs, our optimizations compound at each recursion level. The 37\% overall speedup enables deeper recursion trees and more complex proof compositions within practical time bounds.

\textbf{Zero-Knowledge Virtual Machines (ZKVMs):} Systems like RISC Zero, SP1, and Jolt that emulate general computation benefit from our unified polynomial framework, as they naturally express operations as constraint systems where our batching excels.

\subsection{Implementation and Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:main_performance} presents measurements from Garaga's production Cairo implementation on Starknet, demonstrating that our theoretical predictions (from section \ref{sec:ops:comp-analysis}) closely match real-world performance.

For 3-pairing Miller loop verification (standard in Groth16), our theoretical analysis (Section~\ref{sec:op:comp:our}) for a single bit operation predicted:
\begin{itemize}
\item 47\% constraint reduction (76C $\rightarrow$ 40C per operation)
\item 75\% commitment reduction (48 $\rightarrow$ 12 $\mathbb{F}_p$ elements)
\end{itemize}

The measured results for Miller Loop validate these predictions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{45\% reduction} in field multiplications (14,456 $\rightarrow$ 7,975 MULMOD)
\item \textbf{77\% reduction} in hash operations (3,758 $\rightarrow$ 876 POSEIDON)
\item \textbf{53\% reduction} in total execution cycles (236,382 $\rightarrow$ 110,666)
\end{itemize}

The close alignment validates our polynomial batching analysis. The hash reduction exceeds our prediction because non-zero bit operations save more commitments and also the associated Fiat-Shamir challenge derivations.

\textbf{Calldata and Fiat-Shamir:} For Miller loop operations, POSEIDON hash reductions improve  from 54\% (n=1) to 69\% (n=2) to 77\% (n=3). This occurs because traditional  approaches require $O(k \cdot n)$ commitments where $k$ is the Miller loop length and $n$ is the number of pairings, while our consolidated polynomial approach requires only $O(k)$ commitments regardless of $n$. \subsection{Final Remarks}

This work demonstrates that reimagining classical algorithms through the lens of constraint-based computation models can yield substantial improvements. By treating entire computational phases as unified polynomial relations rather than sequences of field operations, we achieved performance gains that make previously impractical applications feasible.

Our techniques, now deployed in production through Garaga on Starknet, have already enabled new privacy-preserving applications on blockchain platforms. As zero-knowledge proofs become increasingly central to blockchain scalability and privacy, optimizations like ours that dramatically reduce verification costs will be essential for widespread adoption.

The success of our polynomial batching approach suggests that many other cryptographic primitives may benefit from similar reconceptualization in constraint-based environments. We hope this work inspires further research into domain-specific optimizations that exploit the unique characteristics of arithmetized computation models.
